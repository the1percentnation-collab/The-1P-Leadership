## The Reality

It is Sunday night and you are running the conversation you have to have on Tuesday. Not planning it. Running it. The version where it goes badly, where they react the way you are afraid they will, where you say the wrong thing and it costs you something.

By Monday morning you have had that conversation nine times. You will have it once more on Tuesday, for real, and roughly eighty percent of the time it goes fine.

Then you do it again the following week with a different conversation.

The cost here is not the worry itself. It is that you arrive at Tuesday already depleted, having spent two days of capacity on a meeting you were going to handle anyway. People around you see someone who is prepared. What they do not see is what the preparation cost, or how little of it was preparation at all.

## The Shift

Your brain is a prediction engine. It is not primarily reacting to the world, it is constantly forecasting what is about to happen and preparing your body for it. That system is one of the most useful things about being human. It is also only as good as the data it was trained on.

If your early training taught you that conflict ends badly, that being wrong gets punished, that people leave when you disappoint them, then your forecasting system built its model on that. It is not malfunctioning now. It is running accurately on a dataset that stopped describing your life a long time ago.

This is why reassurance never works for long. When someone tells you it will probably be fine, they are arguing with the output. The model is untouched, so it generates the same forecast tomorrow. And the research on uncertainty is clear that for many people, not knowing is more distressing than knowing something bad is coming. Your brain would rather have a terrible prediction than no prediction, so it manufactures one and presents it as insight.

Here is the shift. You cannot argue the forecast down. You can feed the system new data, and that only comes from evidence you collect yourself.

## Do This Now

This is a record, not a mindset exercise. The record is what does the work.

1. Keep a single running note. Every time you catch yourself rehearsing an outcome, write one line: the date, and what you are predicting will happen. Be specific. "He is going to push back hard and I will lose the account" is usable. "It will go badly" is not.
2. After the thing happens, go back and write what actually happened. One line. No analysis.
3. Do not read it for a month. The value is in volume, not in any single entry.
4. At the end of the month, read the whole list in one sitting and count. How many forecasts landed? How many were close? How many were nowhere near?

Most people are shocked by the first month. Not because the worry disappears, but because they are holding the first real evidence they have ever collected about their own accuracy. That is data your prediction engine can actually use. Nobody else's reassurance can be entered into the system. Your own track record can.

Run it for three months and something quieter happens. The forecast still shows up, but it stops carrying the weight of fact. It starts sounding like what it is, which is a guess from a machine with a known error rate.

## One Line to Carry

Stop arguing with the forecast. Start keeping score on it.
